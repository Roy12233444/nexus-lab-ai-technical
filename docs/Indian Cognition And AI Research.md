# **01.03 — Cognition Knowledge Inventory**

## **1\. Research Scope**

This report investigates the foundational mechanics of cognition within classical Indian intellectual traditions, treating cognition as a distinct operational domain separate from epistemology. While previous inquiries focused on how valid knowledge is established and justified, the current scope is rigorously confined to the ontology and mechanics of the cognitive event itself. The objective is to map how cognition arises, operates, persists, alters, and manifests to the cognizing agent across differing philosophical architectures. The analysis prioritizes primary philosophical frameworks—including Nyāya, Navya-Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Advaita Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, Jainism, Buddhist epistemology, and Vyākaraṇa (linguistic traditions)—preserving their native terminologies and inherent ontological disagreements. Only after establishing this historical baseline are cautious hypotheses regarding artificial cognitive architectures and potential Nexus LAB AI integrations investigated, ensuring that computational concepts are not retroactively forced upon classical texts.

## **2\. Central Questions**

The inquiry systematically addresses the fundamental architecture of the cognitive event. It explores the precise definition of cognition and the ontological status of the cognitive event across differing schools. The research delineates the specific conditions and causal collocations required to produce cognition, isolating the respective roles of the mind, the sensory apparatus, and the self or knower. It interrogates the relationship between the cognitive event and its external object, seeking to distinguish direct perception from secondary states such as memory, imagination, and conceptualization. Furthermore, the analysis examines the mechanics of cognitive error, the persistence of cognitive continuity against theories of momentariness, and the functional boundaries separating cognition from volition, emotion, and subsequent action. Finally, the report questions the extent to which these classical cognitive architectures can be legitimately formalized into modern computational frameworks.

## **3\. What Is Cognition?**

In classical Indian philosophy, cognition is a highly fractionated concept, with different intellectual traditions proposing incompatible ontological models for what a cognitive event fundamentally is. There is no single equivalent to the modern English term "cognition," but rather a matrix of precise technical terms denoting distinct processes.  
Within the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika system, cognition (_jñāna_ or _buddhi_) is defined as an adventitious, episodic quality (_guṇa_) that arises within the soul (_Ātman_) only when a specific causal collocation occurs1. This collocation requires the conjunction of the external object, the sense organ, the internal mind (_manas_), and the soul. Cognition is not the essence of the self; the Nyāya self is inherently unconscious and remains so in states such as deep sleep or final liberation. Cognition is entirely intentional—always directed toward an object—and exists as a transient product in time, continually replaced by subsequent cognitions1.  
The Sāṃkhya and Yoga traditions offer a radically different materialist-dualist model. Here, cognition is not a property of the conscious self at all. Instead, it is a material modification (_vṛtti_) of the intellect (_buddhi_ or _citta_), which is an evolute of insentient primordial matter (_Prakṛti_)2. Because the intellect contains a high degree of _sattva_ (the quality of illumination and clarity), it is capable of reflecting the pure, immutable consciousness of the true self (_Puruṣa_). A cognitive event is therefore a mechanical, structural transformation of material energy that only appears to possess consciousness due to the proximity and reflection of the observing *Puruṣa*2.  
Advaita Vedānta introduces a bifurcation between fundamental consciousness (_svarūpa-jñāna_) and empirical cognition (_vṛtti-jñāna_). Fundamental consciousness is the eternal, unchanging reality of Brahman. Empirical cognition, however, is a mechanical process wherein the internal organ (_antaḥkaraṇa_) literally flows out through the sensory channels to envelope the object, assuming its form (_vṛtti_)5. This dynamic modification is then illuminated by the static witness consciousness (_sākṣin_), rendering the object known.  
Conversely, the Buddhist Epistemological school (represented by Dignāga and Dharmakīrti) rejects any enduring substrate. Cognition (_vijñāna_) is defined as a momentary, self-aware event7. There is no soul or mind that "possesses" cognition; rather, the mind is simply the continuous, rapid succession of discrete cognitive moments (_santāna_). Dharmakīrti explicitly defines true perceptual cognition as entirely free from conceptual construction (_kalpanāpoḍham_) and non-erroneous (_abhrāntam_), arguing that it is a direct causal imprint of a unique, momentary particular (_svalakṣaṇa_)8.

## **4\. The Cognizer / Knower**

The concept of the knower (_Pramātṛ_) dictates the architecture of subjectivity across traditions, explicitly questioning whether the knower is a permanent substance, a pure witness, a constructed illusion, or a causal stream.  
The Nyāya school posits the knower as the _Ātman_, a permanent, all-pervading substance. However, this knower is not inherently conscious. It becomes a knowing subject only when conjugated with the atomic mind (_manas_) and the sensory apparatus1. The self is distinct from both the body and the mind, serving as the enduring locus where cognitive qualities accumulate and dissipate.  
Sāṃkhya vehemently rejects the idea that the true self is an active knower. The ultimate self (_Puruṣa_) is a pure, inactive witness (_sākṣin_)2. The empirical knower—the entity that says "I know"—is a constructed subject generated by _ahaṃkāra_ (the ego-principle). This ego-principle is a mechanical evolute of matter that falsely appropriates the cognitive modifications of the intellect, attributing agency and ownership to itself3.  
Advaita Vedānta similarly treats the empirical knower (_jīva_) as a complex construct. It is formed by the reflection of pure, non-dual consciousness within the limiting adjunct of the internal organ and ignorance (_avidyā_)6. The ultimate reality remains the detached witness, entirely separate from the empirical mechanics of knowing.  
Buddhist traditions deconstruct the knower entirely through the doctrine of _Anātman_ (no-self). There is no permanent cognizer; there is only the act of cognition. The sense of an enduring knower is a cognitive illusion produced by the unbroken, causal continuity of momentary mental events. The "knower" is merely a nominal, linguistic convenience applied to this causal stream7.  
In contrast, Jainism asserts that the knower is the _Jīva_, an inherently conscious and permanent substance. Unlike the all-pervading soul of Nyāya, the Jaina soul expands and contracts to perfectly fit the dimensions of the physical body it occupies12. Its intrinsic capacity for omniscience is only restricted by the accumulation of karmic matter, which filters and limits its cognitive reach.

## **5\. Manas**

The term _manas_ is frequently translated as "mind," yet its classical technical function is highly specialized and varies significantly between the Nyāya and Sāṃkhya-Yoga architectures.  
In the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika system, _manas_ operates as an internal sense organ (_antaḥkaraṇa_). Crucially, it is atomic in size, meaning it lacks spatial magnitude1. This atomic nature serves a specific architectural purpose: it acts as a strict processing bottleneck. Because it is atomic, it can only form one conjunction at a time. This explains why, even if all five external senses are stimulated simultaneously, human cognition occurs sequentially1. The _manas_ must rapidly switch connections between the soul, the senses, and stored memories to generate coherent experience. Furthermore, _manas_ is the specific instrument required for the internal perception of subjective states like pleasure, pain, desire, and the apperception of cognition itself14.  
In Sāṃkhya and Yoga, _manas_ is not atomic. It is the third evolute of the internal organ, emerging directly from the ego-principle (_ahaṃkāra_)2. Its role is functional and synthetic. _Manas_ receives raw, disparate data from the five organs of perception and synthesizes them into actionable, cohesive percepts, which it then routes to the organs of action3. It is characterized by _saṃkalpa_ (assimilation, synthesis, and intention)3. It does not make final decisions; it merely structures the data for the higher intellect to evaluate.

## **6\. Buddhi**

Within Sāṃkhya-Yoga, _buddhi_ (frequently termed _Mahat_, or the "Great One") represents the most subtle and primary evolute of primordial matter (_Prakṛti_)15. It must not be conflated with the Western concept of an inherently conscious "intelligence." In Sāṃkhya, _buddhi_ is entirely material and strictly insentient16.  
Its primary cognitive function is _adhyavasāya_—ascertainment, determination, and decisive judgment3. While _manas_ synthesizes sensory data into a cohesive picture, it is the _buddhi_ that executes the definitive judgment (e.g., "This is a dangerous animal; I must run"). Because _buddhi_ contains the highest preponderance of the _sattva_ element (characterized by lucidity and buoyancy), it serves as a mirror, capable of reflecting the light of the true consciousness (_Puruṣa_)2. This reflection animates the _buddhi_, allowing the mechanical processes of determination to appear as though they are conscious, sentient decisions.

## **7\. Citta**

_Citta_ is the foundational cognitive construct in the Yoga traditions of Patañjali, often serving as a comprehensive term for the entire internal organ (subsuming _buddhi_, _ahaṃkāra_, and _manas_).  
The mechanics of _citta_ are defined entirely by its continuous state of fluctuation or modification, known as *vṛttis*17. Patañjali defines the entire enterprise of Yoga as _citta-vṛtti-nirodha_—the active restriction and ultimate cessation of these mental modifications18. The _citta_ operates as a dynamic, self-conditioning repository. Every cognitive event and action leaves a _saṃskāra_ (a latent impression or subliminal trace) within the *citta*18. Over time, these traces cluster by similarity into _vāsanās_ (deep-seated habit patterns and behavioral dispositions). These _vāsanās_ act as latent engines, continually pushing new _vṛttis_ to the surface of awareness, creating a cyclical loop of conditioning, action, and further impression18. It is a highly fluid, karmically driven system that resists simplistic modern analogies to passive memory databases or digital latent spaces, as the _citta_ is fundamentally teleological and tied to spiritual bondage.

## **8\. Consciousness**

The rigorous separation of cognition (the mechanical processing of information) from consciousness (the subjective illumination or witnessing of that information) is a critical feature of Indian philosophy, though the exact relationship is fiercely debated.  
The Nyāya school argues that consciousness (_caitanya_) is merely a property of cognition. It is not fundamental to reality; it is an episodic phenomenon that flares into existence within the soul only when the mechanical conditions for a cognitive event are met1.  
Advaita Vedānta takes the inverse position. Consciousness is the fundamental, non-dual reality (_Brahman_). Cognition is merely a transient, material modification (_vṛtti_) appearing within and illuminated by this immutable consciousness6.  
Sāṃkhya and Yoga maintain a strict dualism. Consciousness (_Puruṣa_) is fundamental, infinite, and eternally distinct from the mind. The mind is a highly evolved mechanical apparatus. Consciousness never acts, thinks, or changes; it merely observes the mechanical permutations of the mind2.  
Buddhist epistemology rejects the premise of an underlying, continuous consciousness entirely. Consciousness is nothing more than the temporal stream of discrete, momentary cognitive flashes. Each flash is self-aware (_svasaṃvedana_) as it arises and ceases, transferring its causal energy to the next moment without any overarching witness overseeing the sequence7.

## **9\. Perception and Cognitive Processing**

The procedural sequences translating external stimuli into conceptualized knowledge demonstrate sophisticated architectural models across the traditions.  
The Nyāya sequence is heavily structured. Initially, there is a physical contact (_sannikarṣa_) between the sense organ and the object14. The atomic mind (_manas_) aligns with the sense organ, connecting it to the soul. This generates the first stage: _Nirvikalpaka Pratyakṣa_ (indeterminate perception)19. This is raw, pre-articulate, non-relational data; the system registers an object and its universal properties, but has not yet linked them (e.g., sensing a shape and a color without synthesizing the concept of a "red pot"). Subsequently, the mind processes this raw data into _Savikalpaka Pratyakṣa_ (determinate perception), applying language and relational structure to form the judgment, "This is a red pot"19. Crucially, Navya-Nyāya adds a metacognitive step: _anuvyavasāya_ (after-cognition), wherein a secondary cognitive event arises to register the primary one, formatting the awareness as "I know that this is a red pot"1.  
The Jaina tradition outlines a four-stage pipeline for empirical cognition (_Mati-jñāna_):

> 1. _Avagraha_ (Sensation): The initial, pre-conscious contact and the very first flicker of indeterminate awareness of a stimulus22.
> 2. _Īhā_ (Speculation/Inquiry): The mind actively questions the raw data, attempting to match it to known categories (e.g., "Is this sound a conch or a horn?")23.
> 3. _Avāya_ (Judgment): The decisive identification and ascertainment of the object ("It is definitively a conch")23.
> 4. _Dhāraṇā_ (Retention): The cognitive judgment is inscribed as a lasting memory trace, ensuring that the perception is not instantly lost and facilitating future recognition23.

## **10\. Attention**

Attention is theorized through the mechanical limits of the cognitive apparatus and the capacity for directed focus.  
In Nyāya, attention is an obligate structural feature resulting from the atomic nature of the _manas_. Because the mind lacks spatial extension, it can physically connect to only one sense organ or memory trace at any given micro-moment. Selection is forced by the architecture; attention is the rapid, sequential routing of the _manas_ to specific inputs1.  
In Yoga, attention is a trainable capacity to control the inherently chaotic fluctuations of the mind. _Dhāraṇā_ (concentration) involves binding the _citta_ to a single locus or object17. This requires the active suppression (_nirodha_) of competing latent impressions (_saṃskāras_) that continuously attempt to surface and distract the mind. Attention here is not merely filtering incoming data; it is the strenuous exertion of will to halt the internal generative mechanisms of the mind.

## **11\. Memory**

Memory (_smṛti_) is universally distinguished from direct perception, as it relies on internal traces rather than immediate contact with external reality.  
Memory mechanics depend on the operation of _saṃskāras_ (latent impressions). When an experience (_anubhava_) occurs, it leaves a subliminal imprint. In Nyāya, this imprint is stored directly in the soul1. Memory is defined as a brand-new cognitive episode triggered when the _manas_ connects with a specific _saṃskāra_ due to associative cues1.  
In Yoga, _smṛti_ is classified as a specific type of mental fluctuation (_vṛtti_) that retrieves a previously experienced object without adding any new characteristics to the original apprehension18. The preservation of these impressions across time forms the basis of behavioral conditioning, with forgetting attributed to the obscuration or overwriting of specific traces by stronger, more recent impressions.

## **12\. Recognition**

Recognition (_pratyabhijñā_) acts as the crucial bridge between direct perception and memory, formalized as the awareness that "This is that same object I saw in the past."  
For Nyāya, recognition is a unified perceptual event. The sense organ contacts the object in the present, but this contact is heavily assisted by a reactivated memory trace. It is not two separate cognitions (a perception and a memory) running in parallel, but a single, complex perception of identity over time20.  
In the Kashmir Śaiva (Pratyabhijñā) traditions, recognition ascends to profound ontological significance. It transcends mere pattern matching. The tradition argues that the individual self is already the universal, absolute consciousness, but this identity is obscured. Spiritual liberation is entirely an act of _pratyabhijñā_—a direct, cognitive realization of one's true identity, akin to suddenly recognizing a disguised person standing right in front of you once a specific cue is provided24.

## **13\. Imagination and Conceptual Cognition**

The boundary between externally grounded cognition and imagination hinges on the role of conceptual construction (_kalpanā_ or _vikalpa_).  
Dharmakīrti and the Buddhist epistemological school argue that true, valid perception is entirely devoid of *kalpanā*8. Conceptualization is a linguistic, generalizing overlay that distorts the raw, momentary particulars (_svalakṣaṇa_) of reality. Concepts operate via the mechanism of _apoha_ (exclusion)25. To conceptualize a "cow" is not to apprehend a positive, universal essence of "cow-ness," but merely to exclude all phenomena that are "non-cows." Therefore, all conceptual cognition relies on a degree of mental construction or imagination, projecting stabilities and universals that do not objectively exist9.  
In Yoga, _vikalpa_ is defined as a mental modification that follows verbal knowledge but is entirely devoid of a corresponding objective reality (for example, conceptualizing "the horns of a hare" or "the consciousness of the intellect")9.

## **14\. Self / Ahaṃkāra / Identity**

The sense of personal identity—the "I"—is analyzed mechanistically by several traditions as a functional cognitive artifact, rather than the ultimate truth of the agent.  
Sāṃkhya isolates _ahaṃkāra_ (the "I-maker") as a highly specific material evolute. Its exclusive function is _abhimāna_ (appropriation or self-arrogation)3. The intellect (_buddhi_) makes an impersonal determination, and the _ahaṃkāra_ subsequently stamps it with self-reference, generating the illusion that "_I_ am perceiving this" or "_I_ am suffering." The true self, _Puruṣa_, remains entirely devoid of "I-ness"4.  
Advaita Vedānta similarly treats the ego as a modification of the internal organ, superimposed upon the immutable witness consciousness due to foundational ignorance6.  
Buddhism abolishes both the ego-construct and the underlying witness. The sense of identity is deemed a cognitive illusion generated by the rapid, causal continuity of the _skandhas_ (aggregates of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness)7. There is no enduring entity that unifies these changing states.

## **15\. Cognition and Language**

The linguistic-philosophical traditions, spearheaded by Bhartṛhari (Vyākaraṇa), contend that cognition is inextricably bound to language. Bhartṛhari posits that there is no cognition devoid of language; all awareness is interpenetrated by words, and conceptualization is fundamentally linguistic26.  
This is formalized in the theory of _Sphoṭa_. When an individual hears a sentence, they do not cognitively assemble the meaning by sequentially adding the definitions of individual words. Instead, the sequential phonetic sounds (_śabda_) act merely as revealing agents. They trigger a holistic, indivisible, instantaneous flash of meaning (_Sphoṭa_) in the mind of the hearer27. Meaning is fundamentally a gestalt phenomenon; the analytical division into separate words and phonemes is an artificial, post-hoc intellectual exercise.

## **16\. Cognitive Error**

Cognitive error (_bhrama_) occurs when the mechanics of perception misfire, a phenomenon deeply debated through various theories of error (_khyātivāda_)29.

- **Anyathākhyāti (Nyāya / Kumārila Mīmāṃsā):** Error is the misapprehension of one real object as another real object (e.g., mistaking a shell for silver). The eye contacts the shell, but due to a flaw (distance, poor lighting), a memory trace of silver is triggered. The mind incorrectly projects the properties of the remembered silver onto the presently perceived shell31.
- **Akhyāti (Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā):** Error is essentially non-apprehension. There is a direct perception of the shell (stripped of its specific "shell-ness") and a memory of silver (stripped of its "past-ness"). The mind simply fails to grasp the distinction between these two separate cognitive events, fusing them in subsequent action21.
- **Anirvacanīyakhyāti (Advaita Vedānta):** The illusory silver is neither entirely real (because it is sublated upon closer inspection) nor entirely unreal (because it is actually experienced and causes a physical reaction). It is an indefinable, temporary creation of ignorance (_avidyā_) residing in the modification of the internal organ (_antaḥkaraṇa-vṛtti_)6.

## **17\. Cognitive Continuity**

Explaining how continuity persists across time relies heavily on the ontological status of the self within each tradition.  
In systems positing an enduring self (Nyāya, Vedānta, Jainism), continuity is guaranteed by the persistence of the underlying substrate (the _Ātman_ or _Jīva_). The impressions (_saṃskāras_) of past actions and cognitions are stored securely within the soul or the mind, effectively bridging past and future experiences1.  
In Sāṃkhya and Yoga, continuity is maintained by the material _Prakṛti_ (specifically the _citta_ or _buddhi_). It is this material intellect that carries the _vāsanās_ (karmic dispositions) across moments and even across lifetimes within the subtle body (_liṅga-śarīra_), ensuring behavioral continuity without requiring the pure consciousness to undergo change3.

## **18\. Momentariness vs Continuity**

The Buddhist doctrine of momentariness (_kṣaṇikavāda_) provides a radical alternative to substrate-based theories of continuity. In this architecture, cognition does not exist _in_ time; it _is_ time. A cognitive event arises, performs its precise function (grasping an object), and ceases completely in a single moment7.  
Continuity is explained strictly through rigorous causal conditioning (_pratītyasamutpāda_). Moment A does not endure or stretch into Moment B. Rather, Moment A's cessation acts as the direct, immediate causal condition for the arising of Moment B. This transfers structural information and karmic momentum forward (analogous to momentum transferring through colliding billiard balls). Consequently, a highly structured, continuous stream of cognition operates effectively without any enduring hardware, soul, or global state manager overarching the process.

## **19\. Cognition, Action and Decision**

Classical Indian frameworks rarely treat cognition as a terminal, isolated event; it is inherently teleological, serving as the necessary precursor to volition and agency. The operational bridge typically follows a rigorous sequence:

> 1. **Cognition (_Jñāna_):** The initial apprehension and determination of an object.
> 2. **Affect / Desire (_Icchā_ / _Dveṣa_):** The evaluation of the object as either conducive to pleasure or productive of pain1.
> 3. **Decision (_Adhyavasāya_ / _Saṃkalpa_):** The determination by the _buddhi_ or _manas_ regarding pursuit or avoidance.
> 4. **Effort / Volition (_Prayatna_):** The energetic impulse leading directly to physical or mental action1.

This sequence demonstrates that cognition is structurally embedded within the organism's drive for survival or spiritual liberation, directly preparing the foundational logic for subsequent inquiries into Agency (Task 01.04).

## **26\. Where the Analogy Breaks**

For every promising comparison between classical cognition and artificial intelligence, the historical and philosophical limits must be explicitly demarcated to prevent distortion.  
**1\. Saṃskāra / Vāsanā as Neural Network Weights / Latent Space**

- _Why the comparison is interesting:_ Both classical traces and AI model weights describe how past experiences are stored as subliminal structures that dictate future outputs without being explicitly "remembered" as episodic events.
- _What genuinely corresponds:_ The mechanism of conditioning. Repetitive patterns strengthen dispositions, altering the probabilistic likelihood of a specific future response.
- _What does NOT correspond:_ Teleology. In Yoga, _saṃskāras_ are driven by _karma_ (moral consequence) and ultimately exist to be eradicated (_nirodha_) to free the self from bondage. AI weights are mathematically optimized toward a loss function devoid of moral or liberatory teleology.
- _Additional research required:_ How to design targeted "unlearning" or "forgetting" mechanisms in AI (analogous to _nirodha_) that effectively neutralize specific _vāsanās_ without causing catastrophic forgetting across the network.

**2\. Ahaṃkāra as AI Self-Model**

- _Why the comparison is interesting:_ Sāṃkhya vigorously separates the "I-maker" (_ahaṃkāra_) from actual intelligence (_buddhi_) and consciousness (_puruṣa_). This perfectly mirrors AI architectures, where a system can be highly intelligent without possessing a self-model.
- _What genuinely corresponds:_ The functional role of appropriation. An AI agent appending "I concluded X" to a decision made by a specialized sub-routine is mathematically and structurally identical to _abhimāna_ (appropriation).
- _What does NOT correspond:_ In Sāṃkhya, _ahaṃkāra_ binds consciousness to suffering and illusion. In AI, a self-model is an intentional, utilitarian engineering feature designed for multi-agent coherence and user interface.
- _Additional research required:_ Engineering an _ahaṃkāra_ module that strictly monitors and tags sub-agent originations, increasing system explainability.

**3\. Nyāya Anuvyavasāya as Metacognition**

- _Why the comparison is interesting:_ It defines the awareness of cognition as a discrete, secondary processing step rather than an inherent, simultaneous property of the first thought.
- _What genuinely corresponds:_ An LLM generating an output (Step 1: _vyavasāya_) and a secondary evaluator model reviewing that output (Step 2: _anuvyavasāya_).
- _What does NOT correspond:_ In Nyāya, both steps belong to the same unitary _Ātman_. In AI, this is frequently split across physically distinct models or API calls.
- _Additional research required:_ Designing unified AI architectures where secondary appraisal (_anuvyavasāya_) operates seamlessly within the same internal inference loop.

## **29\. Research Gaps**

> 1. **Jaina Epistemology:** Source material favored Nyāya and Buddhism; deeper primary source analysis of Jaina _Anekāntavāda_ (multi-perspectivism) is required to understand its specific logic of uncertain cognition.
> 2. **Kashmir Śaivism:** The mechanics of _Pratyabhijñā_ (recognition) are presently understood at a high philosophical level, but the exact cognitive sequences proposed by Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta require further isolation.
> 3. **Mīmāṃsā Hermeneutics:** While Kumārila's defense of realism was noted, the exact mechanics of sentence-cognition (_śābda-bodha_) in Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā (which argues that words only have meaning in the context of action) was insufficiently covered and warrants a dedicated sub-study.
