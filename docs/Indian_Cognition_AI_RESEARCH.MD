01.03 — Cognition Knowledge Inventory
1. Research Scope
This report investigates the foundational mechanics of cognition within classical Indian intellectual traditions, treating cognition as a distinct operational domain separate from epistemology. While previous inquiries focused on how valid knowledge is established and justified, the current scope is rigorously confined to the ontology and mechanics of the cognitive event itself. The objective is to map how cognition arises, operates, persists, alters, and manifests to the cognizing agent across differing philosophical architectures. The analysis prioritizes primary philosophical frameworks—including Nyāya, Navya-Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Advaita Vedānta, Mīmāṃsā, Jainism, Buddhist epistemology, and Vyākaraṇa (linguistic traditions)—preserving their native terminologies and inherent ontological disagreements. Only after establishing this historical baseline are cautious hypotheses regarding artificial cognitive architectures and potential Nexus LAB AI integrations investigated, ensuring that computational concepts are not retroactively forced upon classical texts.
2. Central Questions
The inquiry systematically addresses the fundamental architecture of the cognitive event. It explores the precise definition of cognition and the ontological status of the cognitive event across differing schools. The research delineates the specific conditions and causal collocations required to produce cognition, isolating the respective roles of the mind, the sensory apparatus, and the self or knower. It interrogates the relationship between the cognitive event and its external object, seeking to distinguish direct perception from secondary states such as memory, imagination, and conceptualization. Furthermore, the analysis examines the mechanics of cognitive error, the persistence of cognitive continuity against theories of momentariness, and the functional boundaries separating cognition from volition, emotion, and subsequent action. Finally, the report questions the extent to which these classical cognitive architectures can be legitimately formalized into modern computational frameworks.
3. What Is Cognition?
In classical Indian philosophy, cognition is a highly fractionated concept, with different intellectual traditions proposing incompatible ontological models for what a cognitive event fundamentally is. There is no single equivalent to the modern English term "cognition," but rather a matrix of precise technical terms denoting distinct processes.
Within the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika system, cognition (jñāna or buddhi) is defined as an adventitious, episodic quality (guṇa) that arises within the soul (Ātman) only when a specific causal collocation occurs1. This collocation requires the conjunction of the external object, the sense organ, the internal mind (manas), and the soul. Cognition is not the essence of the self; the Nyāya self is inherently unconscious and remains so in states such as deep sleep or final liberation. Cognition is entirely intentional—always directed toward an object—and exists as a transient product in time, continually replaced by subsequent cognitions1.
The Sāṃkhya and Yoga traditions offer a radically different materialist-dualist model. Here, cognition is not a property of the conscious self at all. Instead, it is a material modification (vṛtti) of the intellect (buddhi or citta), which is an evolute of insentient primordial matter (Prakṛti)2. Because the intellect contains a high degree of sattva (the quality of illumination and clarity), it is capable of reflecting the pure, immutable consciousness of the true self (Puruṣa). A cognitive event is therefore a mechanical, structural transformation of material energy that only appears to possess consciousness due to the proximity and reflection of the observing Puruṣa2.
Advaita Vedānta introduces a bifurcation between fundamental consciousness (svarūpa-jñāna) and empirical cognition (vṛtti-jñāna). Fundamental consciousness is the eternal, unchanging reality of Brahman. Empirical cognition, however, is a mechanical process wherein the internal organ (antaḥkaraṇa) literally flows out through the sensory channels to envelope the object, assuming its form (vṛtti)5. This dynamic modification is then illuminated by the static witness consciousness (sākṣin), rendering the object known.
Conversely, the Buddhist Epistemological school (represented by Dignāga and Dharmakīrti) rejects any enduring substrate. Cognition (vijñāna) is defined as a momentary, self-aware event7. There is no soul or mind that "possesses" cognition; rather, the mind is simply the continuous, rapid succession of discrete cognitive moments (santāna). Dharmakīrti explicitly defines true perceptual cognition as entirely free from conceptual construction (kalpanāpoḍham) and non-erroneous (abhrāntam), arguing that it is a direct causal imprint of a unique, momentary particular (svalakṣaṇa)8.
4. The Cognizer / Knower
The concept of the knower (Pramātṛ) dictates the architecture of subjectivity across traditions, explicitly questioning whether the knower is a permanent substance, a pure witness, a constructed illusion, or a causal stream.
The Nyāya school posits the knower as the Ātman, a permanent, all-pervading substance. However, this knower is not inherently conscious. It becomes a knowing subject only when conjugated with the atomic mind (manas) and the sensory apparatus1. The self is distinct from both the body and the mind, serving as the enduring locus where cognitive qualities accumulate and dissipate.
Sāṃkhya vehemently rejects the idea that the true self is an active knower. The ultimate self (Puruṣa) is a pure, inactive witness (sākṣin)2. The empirical knower—the entity that says "I know"—is a constructed subject generated by ahaṃkāra (the ego-principle). This ego-principle is a mechanical evolute of matter that falsely appropriates the cognitive modifications of the intellect, attributing agency and ownership to itself3.
Advaita Vedānta similarly treats the empirical knower (jīva) as a complex construct. It is formed by the reflection of pure, non-dual consciousness within the limiting adjunct of the internal organ and ignorance (avidyā)6. The ultimate reality remains the detached witness, entirely separate from the empirical mechanics of knowing.
Buddhist traditions deconstruct the knower entirely through the doctrine of Anātman (no-self). There is no permanent cognizer; there is only the act of cognition. The sense of an enduring knower is a cognitive illusion produced by the unbroken, causal continuity of momentary mental events. The "knower" is merely a nominal, linguistic convenience applied to this causal stream7.
In contrast, Jainism asserts that the knower is the Jīva, an inherently conscious and permanent substance. Unlike the all-pervading soul of Nyāya, the Jaina soul expands and contracts to perfectly fit the dimensions of the physical body it occupies12. Its intrinsic capacity for omniscience is only restricted by the accumulation of karmic matter, which filters and limits its cognitive reach.
5. Manas
The term manas is frequently translated as "mind," yet its classical technical function is highly specialized and varies significantly between the Nyāya and Sāṃkhya-Yoga architectures.
In the Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika system, manas operates as an internal sense organ (antaḥkaraṇa). Crucially, it is atomic in size, meaning it lacks spatial magnitude1. This atomic nature serves a specific architectural purpose: it acts as a strict processing bottleneck. Because it is atomic, it can only form one conjunction at a time. This explains why, even if all five external senses are stimulated simultaneously, human cognition occurs sequentially1. The manas must rapidly switch connections between the soul, the senses, and stored memories to generate coherent experience. Furthermore, manas is the specific instrument required for the internal perception of subjective states like pleasure, pain, desire, and the apperception of cognition itself14.
In Sāṃkhya and Yoga, manas is not atomic. It is the third evolute of the internal organ, emerging directly from the ego-principle (ahaṃkāra)2. Its role is functional and synthetic. Manas receives raw, disparate data from the five organs of perception and synthesizes them into actionable, cohesive percepts, which it then routes to the organs of action3. It is characterized by saṃkalpa (assimilation, synthesis, and intention)3. It does not make final decisions; it merely structures the data for the higher intellect to evaluate.
6. Buddhi
Within Sāṃkhya-Yoga, buddhi (frequently termed Mahat, or the "Great One") represents the most subtle and primary evolute of primordial matter (Prakṛti)15. It must not be conflated with the Western concept of an inherently conscious "intelligence." In Sāṃkhya, buddhi is entirely material and strictly insentient16.
Its primary cognitive function is adhyavasāya—ascertainment, determination, and decisive judgment3. While manas synthesizes sensory data into a cohesive picture, it is the buddhi that executes the definitive judgment (e.g., "This is a dangerous animal; I must run"). Because buddhi contains the highest preponderance of the sattva element (characterized by lucidity and buoyancy), it serves as a mirror, capable of reflecting the light of the true consciousness (Puruṣa)2. This reflection animates the buddhi, allowing the mechanical processes of determination to appear as though they are conscious, sentient decisions.
7. Citta
Citta is the foundational cognitive construct in the Yoga traditions of Patañjali, often serving as a comprehensive term for the entire internal organ (subsuming buddhi, ahaṃkāra, and manas).
The mechanics of citta are defined entirely by its continuous state of fluctuation or modification, known as vṛttis17. Patañjali defines the entire enterprise of Yoga as citta-vṛtti-nirodha—the active restriction and ultimate cessation of these mental modifications18. The citta operates as a dynamic, self-conditioning repository. Every cognitive event and action leaves a saṃskāra (a latent impression or subliminal trace) within the citta18. Over time, these traces cluster by similarity into vāsanās (deep-seated habit patterns and behavioral dispositions). These vāsanās act as latent engines, continually pushing new vṛttis to the surface of awareness, creating a cyclical loop of conditioning, action, and further impression18. It is a highly fluid, karmically driven system that resists simplistic modern analogies to passive memory databases or digital latent spaces, as the citta is fundamentally teleological and tied to spiritual bondage.
8. Consciousness
The rigorous separation of cognition (the mechanical processing of information) from consciousness (the subjective illumination or witnessing of that information) is a critical feature of Indian philosophy, though the exact relationship is fiercely debated.
The Nyāya school argues that consciousness (caitanya) is merely a property of cognition. It is not fundamental to reality; it is an episodic phenomenon that flares into existence within the soul only when the mechanical conditions for a cognitive event are met1.
Advaita Vedānta takes the inverse position. Consciousness is the fundamental, non-dual reality (Brahman). Cognition is merely a transient, material modification (vṛtti) appearing within and illuminated by this immutable consciousness6.
Sāṃkhya and Yoga maintain a strict dualism. Consciousness (Puruṣa) is fundamental, infinite, and eternally distinct from the mind. The mind is a highly evolved mechanical apparatus. Consciousness never acts, thinks, or changes; it merely observes the mechanical permutations of the mind2.
Buddhist epistemology rejects the premise of an underlying, continuous consciousness entirely. Consciousness is nothing more than the temporal stream of discrete, momentary cognitive flashes. Each flash is self-aware (svasaṃvedana) as it arises and ceases, transferring its causal energy to the next moment without any overarching witness overseeing the sequence7.
9. Perception and Cognitive Processing
The procedural sequences translating external stimuli into conceptualized knowledge demonstrate sophisticated architectural models across the traditions.
The Nyāya sequence is heavily structured. Initially, there is a physical contact (sannikarṣa) between the sense organ and the object14. The atomic mind (manas) aligns with the sense organ, connecting it to the soul. This generates the first stage: Nirvikalpaka Pratyakṣa (indeterminate perception)19. This is raw, pre-articulate, non-relational data; the system registers an object and its universal properties, but has not yet linked them (e.g., sensing a shape and a color without synthesizing the concept of a "red pot"). Subsequently, the mind processes this raw data into Savikalpaka Pratyakṣa (determinate perception), applying language and relational structure to form the judgment, "This is a red pot"19. Crucially, Navya-Nyāya adds a metacognitive step: anuvyavasāya (after-cognition), wherein a secondary cognitive event arises to register the primary one, formatting the awareness as "I know that this is a red pot"1.
The Jaina tradition outlines a four-stage pipeline for empirical cognition (Mati-jñāna):
Avagraha (Sensation): The initial, pre-conscious contact and the very first flicker of indeterminate awareness of a stimulus22.
Īhā (Speculation/Inquiry): The mind actively questions the raw data, attempting to match it to known categories (e.g., "Is this sound a conch or a horn?")23.
Avāya (Judgment): The decisive identification and ascertainment of the object ("It is definitively a conch")23.
Dhāraṇā (Retention): The cognitive judgment is inscribed as a lasting memory trace, ensuring that the perception is not instantly lost and facilitating future recognition23.
10. Attention
Attention is theorized through the mechanical limits of the cognitive apparatus and the capacity for directed focus.
In Nyāya, attention is an obligate structural feature resulting from the atomic nature of the manas. Because the mind lacks spatial extension, it can physically connect to only one sense organ or memory trace at any given micro-moment. Selection is forced by the architecture; attention is the rapid, sequential routing of the manas to specific inputs1.
In Yoga, attention is a trainable capacity to control the inherently chaotic fluctuations of the mind. Dhāraṇā (concentration) involves binding the citta to a single locus or object17. This requires the active suppression (nirodha) of competing latent impressions (saṃskāras) that continuously attempt to surface and distract the mind. Attention here is not merely filtering incoming data; it is the strenuous exertion of will to halt the internal generative mechanisms of the mind.
11. Memory
Memory (smṛti) is universally distinguished from direct perception, as it relies on internal traces rather than immediate contact with external reality.
Memory mechanics depend on the operation of saṃskāras (latent impressions). When an experience (anubhava) occurs, it leaves a subliminal imprint. In Nyāya, this imprint is stored directly in the soul1. Memory is defined as a brand-new cognitive episode triggered when the manas connects with a specific saṃskāra due to associative cues1.
In Yoga, smṛti is classified as a specific type of mental fluctuation (vṛtti) that retrieves a previously experienced object without adding any new characteristics to the original apprehension18. The preservation of these impressions across time forms the basis of behavioral conditioning, with forgetting attributed to the obscuration or overwriting of specific traces by stronger, more recent impressions.
12. Recognition
Recognition (pratyabhijñā) acts as the crucial bridge between direct perception and memory, formalized as the awareness that "This is that same object I saw in the past."
For Nyāya, recognition is a unified perceptual event. The sense organ contacts the object in the present, but this contact is heavily assisted by a reactivated memory trace. It is not two separate cognitions (a perception and a memory) running in parallel, but a single, complex perception of identity over time20.
In the Kashmir Śaiva (Pratyabhijñā) traditions, recognition ascends to profound ontological significance. It transcends mere pattern matching. The tradition argues that the individual self is already the universal, absolute consciousness, but this identity is obscured. Spiritual liberation is entirely an act of pratyabhijñā—a direct, cognitive realization of one's true identity, akin to suddenly recognizing a disguised person standing right in front of you once a specific cue is provided24.
13. Imagination and Conceptual Cognition
The boundary between externally grounded cognition and imagination hinges on the role of conceptual construction (kalpanā or vikalpa).
Dharmakīrti and the Buddhist epistemological school argue that true, valid perception is entirely devoid of kalpanā8. Conceptualization is a linguistic, generalizing overlay that distorts the raw, momentary particulars (svalakṣaṇa) of reality. Concepts operate via the mechanism of apoha (exclusion)25. To conceptualize a "cow" is not to apprehend a positive, universal essence of "cow-ness," but merely to exclude all phenomena that are "non-cows." Therefore, all conceptual cognition relies on a degree of mental construction or imagination, projecting stabilities and universals that do not objectively exist9.
In Yoga, vikalpa is defined as a mental modification that follows verbal knowledge but is entirely devoid of a corresponding objective reality (for example, conceptualizing "the horns of a hare" or "the consciousness of the intellect")9.
14. Self / Ahaṃkāra / Identity
The sense of personal identity—the "I"—is analyzed mechanistically by several traditions as a functional cognitive artifact, rather than the ultimate truth of the agent.
Sāṃkhya isolates ahaṃkāra (the "I-maker") as a highly specific material evolute. Its exclusive function is abhimāna (appropriation or self-arrogation)3. The intellect (buddhi) makes an impersonal determination, and the ahaṃkāra subsequently stamps it with self-reference, generating the illusion that "I am perceiving this" or "I am suffering." The true self, Puruṣa, remains entirely devoid of "I-ness"4.
Advaita Vedānta similarly treats the ego as a modification of the internal organ, superimposed upon the immutable witness consciousness due to foundational ignorance6.
Buddhism abolishes both the ego-construct and the underlying witness. The sense of identity is deemed a cognitive illusion generated by the rapid, causal continuity of the skandhas (aggregates of form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness)7. There is no enduring entity that unifies these changing states.
15. Cognition and Language
The linguistic-philosophical traditions, spearheaded by Bhartṛhari (Vyākaraṇa), contend that cognition is inextricably bound to language. Bhartṛhari posits that there is no cognition devoid of language; all awareness is interpenetrated by words, and conceptualization is fundamentally linguistic26.
This is formalized in the theory of Sphoṭa. When an individual hears a sentence, they do not cognitively assemble the meaning by sequentially adding the definitions of individual words. Instead, the sequential phonetic sounds (śabda) act merely as revealing agents. They trigger a holistic, indivisible, instantaneous flash of meaning (Sphoṭa) in the mind of the hearer27. Meaning is fundamentally a gestalt phenomenon; the analytical division into separate words and phonemes is an artificial, post-hoc intellectual exercise.
16. Cognitive Error
Cognitive error (bhrama) occurs when the mechanics of perception misfire, a phenomenon deeply debated through various theories of error (khyātivāda)29.
Anyathākhyāti (Nyāya / Kumārila Mīmāṃsā): Error is the misapprehension of one real object as another real object (e.g., mistaking a shell for silver). The eye contacts the shell, but due to a flaw (distance, poor lighting), a memory trace of silver is triggered. The mind incorrectly projects the properties of the remembered silver onto the presently perceived shell31.
Akhyāti (Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā): Error is essentially non-apprehension. There is a direct perception of the shell (stripped of its specific "shell-ness") and a memory of silver (stripped of its "past-ness"). The mind simply fails to grasp the distinction between these two separate cognitive events, fusing them in subsequent action21.
Anirvacanīyakhyāti (Advaita Vedānta): The illusory silver is neither entirely real (because it is sublated upon closer inspection) nor entirely unreal (because it is actually experienced and causes a physical reaction). It is an indefinable, temporary creation of ignorance (avidyā) residing in the modification of the internal organ (antaḥkaraṇa-vṛtti)6.
17. Cognitive Continuity
Explaining how continuity persists across time relies heavily on the ontological status of the self within each tradition.
In systems positing an enduring self (Nyāya, Vedānta, Jainism), continuity is guaranteed by the persistence of the underlying substrate (the Ātman or Jīva). The impressions (saṃskāras) of past actions and cognitions are stored securely within the soul or the mind, effectively bridging past and future experiences1.
In Sāṃkhya and Yoga, continuity is maintained by the material Prakṛti (specifically the citta or buddhi). It is this material intellect that carries the vāsanās (karmic dispositions) across moments and even across lifetimes within the subtle body (liṅga-śarīra), ensuring behavioral continuity without requiring the pure consciousness to undergo change3.
18. Momentariness vs Continuity
The Buddhist doctrine of momentariness (kṣaṇikavāda) provides a radical alternative to substrate-based theories of continuity. In this architecture, cognition does not exist in time; it is time. A cognitive event arises, performs its precise function (grasping an object), and ceases completely in a single moment7.
Continuity is explained strictly through rigorous causal conditioning (pratītyasamutpāda). Moment A does not endure or stretch into Moment B. Rather, Moment A's cessation acts as the direct, immediate causal condition for the arising of Moment B. This transfers structural information and karmic momentum forward (analogous to momentum transferring through colliding billiard balls). Consequently, a highly structured, continuous stream of cognition operates effectively without any enduring hardware, soul, or global state manager overarching the process.
19. Cognition, Action and Decision
Classical Indian frameworks rarely treat cognition as a terminal, isolated event; it is inherently teleological, serving as the necessary precursor to volition and agency. The operational bridge typically follows a rigorous sequence:
Cognition (Jñāna): The initial apprehension and determination of an object.
Affect / Desire (Icchā / Dveṣa): The evaluation of the object as either conducive to pleasure or productive of pain1.
Decision (Adhyavasāya / Saṃkalpa): The determination by the buddhi or manas regarding pursuit or avoidance.
Effort / Volition (Prayatna): The energetic impulse leading directly to physical or mental action1.
This sequence demonstrates that cognition is structurally embedded within the organism's drive for survival or spiritual liberation, directly preparing the foundational logic for subsequent inquiries into Agency (Task 01.04).

26. Where the Analogy Breaks
For every promising comparison between classical cognition and artificial intelligence, the historical and philosophical limits must be explicitly demarcated to prevent distortion.
1. Saṃskāra / Vāsanā as Neural Network Weights / Latent Space
Why the comparison is interesting: Both classical traces and AI model weights describe how past experiences are stored as subliminal structures that dictate future outputs without being explicitly "remembered" as episodic events.
What genuinely corresponds: The mechanism of conditioning. Repetitive patterns strengthen dispositions, altering the probabilistic likelihood of a specific future response.
What does NOT correspond: Teleology. In Yoga, saṃskāras are driven by karma (moral consequence) and ultimately exist to be eradicated (nirodha) to free the self from bondage. AI weights are mathematically optimized toward a loss function devoid of moral or liberatory teleology.
Additional research required: How to design targeted "unlearning" or "forgetting" mechanisms in AI (analogous to nirodha) that effectively neutralize specific vāsanās without causing catastrophic forgetting across the network.
2. Ahaṃkāra as AI Self-Model
Why the comparison is interesting: Sāṃkhya vigorously separates the "I-maker" (ahaṃkāra) from actual intelligence (buddhi) and consciousness (puruṣa). This perfectly mirrors AI architectures, where a system can be highly intelligent without possessing a self-model.
What genuinely corresponds: The functional role of appropriation. An AI agent appending "I concluded X" to a decision made by a specialized sub-routine is mathematically and structurally identical to abhimāna (appropriation).
What does NOT correspond: In Sāṃkhya, ahaṃkāra binds consciousness to suffering and illusion. In AI, a self-model is an intentional, utilitarian engineering feature designed for multi-agent coherence and user interface.
Additional research required: Engineering an ahaṃkāra module that strictly monitors and tags sub-agent originations, increasing system explainability.
3. Nyāya Anuvyavasāya as Metacognition
Why the comparison is interesting: It defines the awareness of cognition as a discrete, secondary processing step rather than an inherent, simultaneous property of the first thought.
What genuinely corresponds: An LLM generating an output (Step 1: vyavasāya) and a secondary evaluator model reviewing that output (Step 2: anuvyavasāya).
What does NOT correspond: In Nyāya, both steps belong to the same unitary Ātman. In AI, this is frequently split across physically distinct models or API calls.
Additional research required: Designing unified AI architectures where secondary appraisal (anuvyavasāya) operates seamlessly within the same internal inference loop.
27. Open Research Questions
How can the Jain pipeline of mati-jñāna (sensation -> speculation -> judgment -> retention) pragmatically inform the mitigation of early-layer hallucinations in deep learning models by forcing delayed categorization?
If we adopt the Sāṃkhya structural distinction between buddhi (decisive intellect) and manas (synthesizing mind), how should future cognitive architectures physically separate data-fusion layers from final-decision gating layers?
Does Dharmakīrti's apoha (meaning purely by exclusion) offer a mathematically viable alternative to distance-based similarity metrics in vector embeddings for category learning?

29. Research Gaps
Jaina Epistemology: Source material favored Nyāya and Buddhism; deeper primary source analysis of Jaina Anekāntavāda (multi-perspectivism) is required to understand its specific logic of uncertain cognition.
Kashmir Śaivism: The mechanics of Pratyabhijñā (recognition) are presently understood at a high philosophical level, but the exact cognitive sequences proposed by Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta require further isolation.
Mīmāṃsā Hermeneutics: While Kumārila's defense of realism was noted, the exact mechanics of sentence-cognition (śābda-bodha) in Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā (which argues that words only have meaning in the context of action) was insufficiently covered and warrants a dedicated sub-study.
